Privacy

Atmos Gallery runs in your browser. Your images are never uploaded, your palettes are never held on a server, and a shared link tells us nothing about who opened it.

Last updated: 27 July 2026 · Data controller: KauStudio ApS

on this page

Your images never leave your device

The palette is extracted on your own machine — the image is read into a canvas, clustered in OKLCH, and discarded. It is never uploaded. In this public build, the code that could send an image isn't merely switched off: it isn't part of the build at all.

Your palettes are stored only in your browser

The archive lives in this browser's local storage. There is no server holding it, no account attached to it, and no way for us to see it. Clearing your browser data deletes it — which is why you can save a project file and keep your own copy, on your own disk.

That archive is not the only thing the tool keeps locally, so here is everything, in full. All of it is first-party, none of it identifies you, and none of it is ever sent anywhere:

  • palette-generator/feed — the archive itself: your palettes.
  • palette-generator/derived — a cache of work already done, so a palette you have opened before doesn't have to be recomputed.
  • palette-generator/landing — a single flag remembering that you have dismissed the opening screen, so it doesn't greet you every visit.
  • palette-generator/pagesize — how many palettes you chose to show per page.
  • palette-generator/loader-session — a flag, for this browser session only, remembering that you have already seen the loading sequence.

Clearing your browser data clears all five. The names begin with palette-generator because they predate the product being called Atmos Gallery, and renaming them would orphan archives that already exist on people's machines.

Share links carry the palette, not a lookup

A shared link encodes the palette in the part of the URL after the #. Browsers never send that part to a server, so opening a shared link doesn't tell us — or anyone else — that you opened it, or what was in it.

No accounts, no ads, no cross-site tracking

There is nothing to sign up for, no cookies at all, and nothing sold or shared with anyone. Two measurement tools run on the page — Vercel Web Analytics, to see how many people visit, and Vercel Speed Insights, to see whether pages load quickly. Both are cookieless and aggregated, neither identifies you, neither follows you to other sites, and neither sees anything you do inside the tool.

What we can see

The site is hosted on Vercel, which keeps standard access logs for the files it serves. Those logs include your IP address and browser user-agent, because a web server cannot answer a request without them.

On top of that, the two measurement tools report — and this is the full list Vercel publishes, not a summary of it:

  • Web Analytics — the time, the page and its route, the referring site, filtered query parameters, your device type, browser and version, operating system and version, and an approximate location that Vercel resolves as far as city level.
  • Speed Insights — the page and its route, the standard Core Web Vitals and which element on the page they attach to, your rough connection speed, device type, browser, operating system, and your country only.

Web Analytics tells one visit from another using a hash derived from the incoming request, which Vercel discards after 24 hours. Speed Insights is documented as carrying no visitor identifier at all. Neither is a cookie, neither writes anything to your device, and neither can recognise you on another site.

Nothing about your images, your palettes, or your archive: those never leave your browser in the first place.

About interpretation

Some environments provide a model that can read an image's mood directly. Where that's available, a small downscaled thumbnail — roughly 320 px on its longest edge, scaled up for high-density displays — is sent for that reading and is not stored. This public build doesn't include that path — palettes are named by a reading that runs locally, on your device.

Our legal basis

We rely on legitimate interests — serving the site reliably and securely, knowing roughly how many people use it, and finding out when it has become slow. We do not ask for consent for this because we are not doing anything a visitor would not expect from a website that simply loads; if you would rather not be counted at all, a tracker-blocking extension will stop both scripts and the tool will work exactly as before.

Who else touches it

One company: Vercel Inc., which hosts the site and provides both measurement tools, acting as our processor. That means your data reaches servers in the United States.

Vercel publishes its current list of sub-processors at security.vercel.com. If you want to know what contractual protections apply to that transfer before you use the tool, write to hello@kau.studio and we will tell you where it stands rather than point you at a document.

There is no one else. No advertising network, no third-party analytics, no email or CRM tool, no embedded video or font service — the fonts and animation libraries are served from this domain, not a CDN.

How long it is kept

We keep no copies ourselves and maintain no database of visitors. Everything below is held by Vercel, and we can neither extend it nor reach into it to find an individual visitor.

The one figure that is published, and the one that matters most: the hash Web Analytics uses to tell visits apart is discarded after 24 hours. After that there is nothing left that could group your page views together, even in principle.

What remains is aggregate — counts by page, country, browser and device, with no thread running back to a person. We have not been able to establish a fixed published period for those aggregates or for Vercel's standard access logs, so rather than invent one, here is the criterion we hold ourselves to: we ask for no retention beyond what the hosting and these two aggregate measurements need, we add no storage of our own on top, and if Vercel publishes a defined period we will state it here rather than leave this paragraph as it is.

Your palettes are a separate matter, and simpler: they are on your machine, for as long as you keep them there. See Your palettes are stored only in your browser.
