Colour Begins as Atmosphere

Start with an image.
Atmos turns its atmosphere into a palette.

Atmos draws five colours from an image and shows how much of it each one covers. It describes each colour by its lightness, chroma and hue, measures the contrast of all ten pairs, and suggests roles such as background, text and accent.

The sections below explain each step and how to read the results.

1.1Start with Colours You Like

A project often starts with an image whose colours feel right. Choosing which colours to take into your design is the next step. If you plan to use them for text and backgrounds, you also need to check their contrast.

Atmos does that first step for you and measures the result. You still choose which colours to use, and adjust them in your own design tools.

A palette is more useful when you can understand the relationships within it.

Atmos brings colour properties, proportions and contrast together, helping you explore how the colours could work while keeping the original image in view.

1.2How Atmos Reads Colour

Atmos uses OKLCH to describe each colour’s lightness, chroma and hue. It also calculates contrast between colour pairs and checks the results against WCAG contrast requirements.

These help you make different decisions: compare the colours in your palette, then check the combinations you want to use for text or interface elements. Colours that look good together may still lack enough contrast for their intended use.

1.3Reading OKLCH

OKLCH describes each colour through three properties: lightness, chroma and hue. Use them to compare the colours in your palette.

Lightness

0.00.20.40.60.81.0

How light or dark a colour appears. Atmos shows this on a scale from 0 to 1, where 0 is black and 1 is white. OKLCH lightness is different from the relative luminance used to calculate WCAG contrast.

Chroma

0.000.040.080.120.160.20

How muted or vivid a colour appears. At zero, the colour is neutral: black, grey or white, depending on its lightness. Higher values move further from neutral. The visible effect also depends on lightness and hue.

Hue

0°30°60°90°120°150°180°210°240°270°300°330°

The colour family, such as red, yellow, green or blue, expressed as a position around a colour wheel. It is measured in degrees, with 0° and 360° marking the same position. Hue becomes less noticeable as chroma approaches zero.

1.4Reading WCAG Contrast

Contrast compares two colours, such as text and its background. Atmos calculates their contrast ratio using the WCAG formula for relative luminance. Results range from 1 to 1 for equal luminance to 21 to 1 for black and white.

The contrast you need depends on how you use the pair. At WCAG AA, normal text requires at least 4.5 to 1 and large text at least 3 to 1. Visual details needed to identify controls or understand graphics generally require 3 to 1 against adjacent colours.

Pairs below 3 to 1 can still be used for decoration where the colour difference is not needed to read content, identify a control or understand information.

Meeting a contrast requirement does not make the whole design accessible. Check each pair in the context where you use it.

2.1Small Details Can Stand Out

The colours that cover the largest areas of an image are not always the ones that catch your attention. A small, vivid detail can stand out against a muted background, while a dark shadow can make lighter colours more noticeable.

Atmos shows the proportions, colour properties and contrast of the extracted palette. Use these to explore the colours and decide which ones to emphasise in your design.

2.2Similar Colours, Different Possibilities

Colours with similar hues can have different uses in a design. Differences in lightness can help separate text from its background or distinguish one surface from another. Differences in chroma can make one colour stand out against more muted colours.

The examples below focus on hue, lightness and chroma in turn. Use them to compare the colours, then check contrast between the pairs you want to use together.

3.1Find a Role for Each Colour

A palette describes the colours drawn from an image. Suggested roles offer possible ways to use those colours in a design.

Atmos suggests background, surface, text, primary, secondary and accent roles. These are a starting point for exploration, not a finished design or a guarantee that every pairing meets a contrast requirement.

Review the roles in the context of your work. Check contrast between the colours you use together, and adjust them in your own tools where needed.

3.2Contrast Creates Hierarchy

Contrast helps you decide which colour pairs can carry text or essential visual information, and which are better suited to decorative use.

Small differences between colours can create subtle depth. Text and essential controls need sufficient contrast against their backgrounds. Check each pairing for its intended use.

3.4Take Your Palette into Your Design

The palette is a starting point for your work. Explore its relationships and decide which colours to carry into your design.

Use harmonies to explore related colours and the contrast checker to assess specific pairs. Keep related palettes together in Library projects, then copy the values or export them for your design tools. You can include six suggested roles as an optional starting structure.

Make adjustments in your own tools and check contrast again when a pairing changes. Back up your Library to keep a separate copy of your saved palettes.
