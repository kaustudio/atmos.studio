Colour Begins as Atmosphere

Start with an image.
Atmos turns its atmosphere into a colour system.

Before colour becomes a value, a token or a role, it is something we experience. The warmth of late-afternoon light. The cool distance of a horizon. A bright surface held against a quiet shadow. Every image contains a particular balance of colour, light and contrast. That balance is what gives it a mood.

Atmos begins there. It reads the visual character of an image, then translates it into a colour system made for use.

1.1From Visual Instinct to a Usable System

Designers often know how something should feel before they know which colours will create it. The reference might be a photograph, a material, a landscape or a moment of light. Its atmosphere can be immediate, but translating that feeling into a working system is rarely straightforward.

Atmos is not a replacement for judgement. It gives that judgement a clearer starting point. The relationships are made visible. The roles are made explicit. Every adjustment remains open to the designer.

Sampling can produce colours. It does not explain which ones matter, how they relate or what each one should do.

A contrast checker can validate a pair. It does not preserve the character of the image that inspired it.

Atmos connects those decisions. It helps designers move from reference to system without losing the reason the reference was chosen in the first place.

1.2How Atmos Reads Colour

Atmos uses two complementary ways of reading colour. OKLCH describes the character of each colour. WCAG measures whether two colours create enough contrast for a particular purpose.

Neither provides the full answer alone. A palette can feel coherent and still contain pairings that are difficult to read. A pair can meet a contrast requirement and still feel disconnected from the atmosphere around it.

Atmos uses both.

1.3Reading OKLCH

Every sampled colour is described through lightness, chroma and hue. Together they reveal the shape of a colour. They do not determine whether it is accessible.

Lightness

0.00.20.40.60.81.0

How light or dark a colour appears, on a scale from 0 to 1. Zero is black. One is white. This is perceptual lightness, which helps designers make changes that feel visually consistent. It is not the same measurement as the relative luminance used by WCAG.

Chroma

0.000.040.080.120.160.20

How strongly a colour departs from neutral. At zero the colour is grey. As chroma rises the colour becomes more vivid. A chroma value only makes sense as part of the complete colour, because its visible effect depends on lightness, hue and the available gamut.

Hue

0°30°60°90°120°150°180°210°240°270°300°330°

The colour’s position around a perceptual colour wheel, measured as an angle from 0° to 360°. As chroma approaches zero, hue carries less visible meaning. A nearly neutral grey can still have a recorded hue, but that hue contributes very little to what the eye sees.

1.4Reading WCAG Contrast

Contrast belongs to a pair, not to an individual colour. WCAG compares the relative luminance of a foreground colour and the colour behind it. The result sits between 1 to 1 and 21 to 1. A ratio of 1 to 1 means there is no luminance contrast. A ratio of 21 to 1 is the maximum contrast between black and white.

A result below 3 to 1 does not make the colours unusable. The pair can still create atmosphere, depth and decorative separation. It should not be responsible for legibility, interaction or essential visual information.

A pair does not simply pass WCAG. It passes for body text, for large text or for a meaningful non-text element.

2.1An Image Is More Than Its Most Common Colours

Conventional colour extraction often begins with frequency. It identifies the colours that occupy the largest areas of an image and ranks them by prevalence.

But prevalence is not the same as importance. A small highlight can define an image’s energy. A near-black shadow can create its depth. A muted field can make a single saturated detail feel vivid.

Atmos considers how much of each colour is present, how the colours relate and what each one contributes to the image as a whole.

2.2Reading the Shape of a Palette

Two colours can share a hue and still perform entirely different roles. A change in lightness can separate one surface from another. It can also determine whether text can be read at all.

A change in chroma can turn a quiet detail into a focal point without changing its underlying hue.

Atmos reads all three dimensions together. Each is taken on its own below, in the palette whose character turns on it.

3.1From Sampled Colours to Purposeful Roles

Colour becomes useful when it has a job. Up to here Atmos has produced a palette: five colours the image gave, with their measurements and the intervals between them. A colour system is that palette once every colour has a role and those relationships have been tested. This section is where one becomes the other.

Atmos translates the visual character of an image into functional roles such as background, surface, text, primary, secondary and accent. The image provides the atmosphere. OKLCH preserves its relationships. The roles provide the structure. WCAG tests whether those roles work together.

The system keeps the feeling of its source while becoming practical enough to use in an interface, identity or digital product.

With five sampled colours and six functional roles, one colour must serve more than one purpose. In Nightfall, the primary and secondary roles share a swatch. Their difference must therefore come from context, scale, placement or treatment rather than colour alone.

3.2Contrast Creates Hierarchy

Compliance is not something added after the palette has been designed. Contrast shapes which relationships can carry content and which should remain atmospheric.

A strong system needs quiet relationships as well as accessible ones. Backgrounds and surfaces may use restrained intervals to create depth. Text and essential controls need stronger separation because they carry information or action.

3.4The Intervals Matter

The character of a palette often lives in the distance between its colours. A narrow change in OKLCH lightness can create subtle depth. A wider one can establish hierarchy. Related hues can produce harmony, while a controlled shift in chroma can introduce tension and focus.

Atmos makes these intervals visible. It reveals which colours belong together and why they feel connected.

3.5Refine the System Without Losing Its Atmosphere

A generated system is a beginning, not a fixed answer. Strengthen a contrast. Quiet a surface. Shift an accent. Change the balance between hierarchy and harmony.

OKLCH makes those adjustments more controlled. One dimension can move while the others remain stable. WCAG verifies whether the adjusted relationship now meets its intended role. Atmos keeps the reasoning visible throughout, so the designer can see what changed, why it changed and which part of the original colour was preserved.
